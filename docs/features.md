# Milton's Properties — What Makes It Different

Feature set derived from what renters and buyers actually complain about, filtered through what the law lets us build.

---

## The strategic thesis

The obvious move is to treat compliance as a tax on the product. The better move is to notice that the compliance work generates assets nobody else is using.

You are already legally required to take timestamped move-in photos, to disclose screening criteria in writing before charging a fee, to process applications in a provable order, to hand over report copies within seven days, to offer rent reporting, and to produce an itemized deposit statement with receipts. Every competitor treats those as paperwork and buries them. Each one is a feature waiting to be surfaced.

That's the wedge: **Milton's is the only platform where the tenant can see the same record the landlord sees.** Not a nicer UI on the same opacity — actual symmetry. Every feature below comes out of that.

It also happens to be the only durable moat available to a solo operator. Anyone can copy a listing page. Copying a posture of radical transparency requires a competitor to give up revenue they're currently earning from opacity, which incumbents structurally can't do.

## What renters actually say is wrong

From the research, ranked by how often it surfaced:

| Pain | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Upfront cash — application fees plus deposit | Fannie Mae's renter survey names it a top challenge alongside affordability itself |
| Applying blind and losing the fee | Fees are charged before anyone tells you whether you'd qualify |
| Deposit disputes at move-out | One of the most common reasons landlords and tenants end up in court; wear-and-tear vs. damage is the flashpoint |
| Listings that don't show the actual unit | 47% call missing unit-specific photos a deal-breaker; 99% say unit-level detail matters; 63% want floor plans |
| Renting sight-unseen with no way to verify | 43% say they're likely to rent unseen; 41% are moving to another city or state |
| Rent that builds nothing | Years of on-time payments produce no credit history |
| Repairs that go unaddressed | Consistently the top category in tenant complaint data |
| Suspicion the process was rigged | Black and Latino renters report materially higher rates of rejection, unexpected fees, and voucher problems |

And one that renters feel without knowing the cause: **fraud is making their terms worse.** Roughly one in eight financial documents submitted with rental applications has been altered; more than 70% of fraud surfaces only after move-in; each case runs about $15,000 to clean up. Landlords respond with bigger deposits, higher income multiples, and cosigner requirements that didn't exist three years ago. Honest applicants pay for it.

## The features

### 1. Free pre-qualification, before any fee — *ships in the prototype*

Enter income, rent target, and a self-reported credit band. Get an immediate meets / doesn't meet against that unit's posted criteria. **No fee, no consumer report, no credit inquiry** — it's arithmetic against published numbers, which keeps it entirely outside FCRA.

This attacks the single most-cited renter grievance directly: paying to find out you never had a chance. Every incumbent could build it in a week. None will, because application fees are their revenue. Milton's earns on managed doors, so it can give this away — and the fee it forgoes buys goodwill from exactly the applicants who would otherwise have wasted their money.

Pair it with a **queue position** display: "You're applicant #3. #1 and #2 are being processed ahead of you." AB 2493 already requires the ordering. Showing it kills the suspicion of a rigged process, which is precisely what applicants from marginalized groups report experiencing.

### 2. The Deposit Meter — *ships in the prototype*

**This is the signature feature. Build it first, name it, market on it.**

Move-in photos are already mandatory under AB 2801, so you already hold a timestamped condition baseline. Turn it into a running ledger. Every fixture — flooring, paint, appliances, counters, fixtures — carries its condition at move-in, its age, and its depreciation schedule. Every maintenance ticket, inspection photo and repair receipt attaches to a specific item.

The tenant sees, at any moment, what their deposit would return today and exactly why each deduction exists. When they move out, the §1950.5 itemized statement generates itself from the ledger with receipts already attached.

The depreciation math is what makes it work. A landlord cannot charge replacement cost for a carpet that was already six years into an eight-year life. Almost every deposit dispute is really an argument about that, conducted without numbers. Showing the schedule from day one ends the argument before it starts.

What it does for each side: the tenant stops fearing a mystery deduction and starts protecting their own money, because they can see the meter move. The owner gets a defensible file that wins in small claims and, more often, avoids it. Nothing else on the market does this, and the data it needs is data you're already required to collect.

### 3. Rent reporting, free — *and you're already required to offer it*

AB 2747 (Civil Code §1954.07), effective April 1 2025, extends the rent-reporting mandate beyond subsidized housing to landlords with 16 or more units — **or fifteen or fewer if corporately owned.** Milton's holds six doors under an LLC, so the obligation already applies.

The law lets you charge the lesser of $10/month or actual cost. Charge nothing. It's a small real expense that turns a compliance chore into the most-loved feature in the product: a pilot by the Credit Builders Alliance found 80% of participating renters raised their scores, by an average of 23 points.

The strategic payoff is a loop nobody else has closed. A tenant whose credit improves inside Milton's becomes a qualified applicant for the next Milton's unit, then eventually a mortgage-ready buyer your realtor represents. Turnover is the number one landlord problem — 42% name it their biggest challenge, well ahead of maintenance. This is a retention engine disguised as a giveaway.

Two honesty requirements: late and missed payments get reported too, and opting out locks you out for six months. Both must be stated plainly at the opt-in, not buried.

### 4. Portable report, issued outward

AB 2559 already forces you to *accept* a portable screening report under 30 days old. Milton's issues one *out*: after screening, the applicant owns a reusable report they can take to any other landlord for 30 days.

It is genuinely counterintuitive — you're helping someone you may have just rejected. That's what makes it worth doing. It costs almost nothing, it means a rejection doesn't cost the applicant another $66 down the street, and it makes "we're not trying to farm you for fees" a claim you can actually demonstrate.

### 5. The visible fee ledger

AB 2493 requires refunding unused screening fees within seven days and delivering the credit report within seven days. Most landlords handle this badly or not at all.

Show a live line: *$66 collected — $38 paid to the screening agency — $28 returning to you on August 2.* An obligation everyone else treats as an accounting annoyance becomes a proof of honesty that costs you nothing you weren't already required to pay.

### 6. Unit-truth listings, including the defects

Since 43% will rent sight-unseen and 47% treat missing unit photos as disqualifying, the listing standard should be: photographs of *that unit*, date-stamped, never the model unit. Floor plan with measured rooms. A single unedited phone walkthrough. Window orientation and real light at three times of day. Cell signal by carrier. Measured street noise.

Then the part nobody does: **a published defect list.** The radiator knocks in January. The second bedroom gets no morning light. Street parking is genuinely hard on Tuesdays. Volunteering flaws is the strongest trust signal available to a small operator, it filters out applicants who'd have left in six months, and it quietly protects you at move-out because nothing on that list can be charged to the tenant later.

### 7. Bank-verified income instead of uploaded paystubs

With roughly one in eight submitted financial documents altered, and fraud driving up terms for honest renters, verified income through a bank connection is stronger evidence than any PDF.

Frame the benefit where the renter can feel it: because verification is stronger, Milton's can advertise a **lower income multiple and no cosigner requirement**. An anti-fraud control sold as a tenant benefit — which is what it actually is.

Keep a manual document path for anyone who won't or can't link a bank account. Verification convenience can't become a barrier.

### 8. Maintenance with a visible clock

Repair delays are the most persistent complaint in tenant data. Give every ticket a posted response window, the assigned vendor's name, and an ETA both sides can see. Add a pre-approved self-repair allowance: fix something under a set dollar amount, upload the receipt, get it credited against rent.

Every resolved ticket also writes to the Deposit Meter, so the tenant watches maintenance history accumulate as evidence in their own favor.

### 9. Vouchers welcome, stated on the listing

Source-of-income discrimination is illegal in California under Gov. Code §12955, and voucher problems show up disproportionately in the experience of Black and Latino renters. Make "housing vouchers accepted" a default badge and a search filter rather than something an applicant has to ask about and brace for.

Costs nothing. Says something. And it widens your applicant pool in a market where vacancy is expensive.

### 10. The buyer's true-cost sheet — *ships in the prototype*

Every for-sale listing carries a full carry-cost projection instead of a monthly-payment estimate:

- Mortgage at today's actual rate, with 3%, 10% and 20% down side by side — because roughly a fifth of buyers wish someone had told them about assistance programs and 13% didn't know 20% wasn't required
- **Property tax at the reassessed value**, not the seller's Prop 13 basis. In California this is the single most brutal surprise, and it's arithmetic anyone can do and almost nobody does before offering
- Insurance at real California numbers, fire risk noted
- HOA dues with three years of increase history and the reserve study status
- Utilities from actual historical bills for the unit
- A maintenance reserve derived from the age of the roof, HVAC and water heater — not a percentage rule of thumb

Then a **first-year cash reality line**: closing costs plus expected early repairs. Given that 73% of buyers get hit with something unexpected inside six months and 54% inside 90 days, this is the number that would have changed their decision.

Beside it, an honest escrow timeline showing each party's real deadlines — buyers expecting a two-week close against a 30-to-40-day reality need that gap named early, not discovered at day 25.

This is factual disclosure with sourced inputs, presented as a calculator. It is not financial advice and shouldn't be dressed as it.

## Build order

**Now, into the existing prototype:** free pre-qualification, queue position, Deposit Meter, buyer true-cost sheet. All four are pure product with no vendor dependency and no legal review beyond what you've already scoped.

**Phase 2:** rent reporting (you're required to offer it anyway), the visible fee ledger, unit-truth listing standard with the defect list, vouchers-welcome badge.

**Phase 3, alongside the screening integration:** bank-verified income, portable outbound reports.

**Phase 4:** maintenance clock and self-repair credits, wired into the Deposit Meter.

---

## The three that actually differentiate

If you only ship three, ship these:

1. **The Deposit Meter.** No competitor has it, the underlying data is legally mandated so you're collecting it regardless, and it dissolves the most common source of landlord-tenant litigation.
2. **Free pre-qualification.** Incumbents can't copy it without giving up application-fee revenue.
3. **The buyer's true-cost sheet with reassessed property tax.** The number that causes the most post-purchase regret, shown before the offer.

Everything else is good. Those three are the product.
